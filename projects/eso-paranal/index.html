ESO - Paranal Armazones

The European Southern Observatory, or ESO for short (European Southern Observatory), is an intergovernmental organization responsible for designing, building, and operating some of the largest telescopes in the world. It is made up of 16 European member states, with Chile as the host country.

ESO’s telescopes are located in the Atacama Desert, a place with unique geographical conditions for astronomical observations. Currently, ESO operates in three sites in Chile: Paranal, La Silla, and Chajnantor (ALMA). In addition, it is building the Extremely Large Telescope (ELT), which will be the largest telescope in the world, on Cerro Armazones—just 23 km away from Cerro Paranal. This telescope could become the first one to find evidence of life beyond our solar system.

At the moment, I work as a Data Engineer in the Paranal software group, as part of the team developing the observatory’s new data platform. This platform will support Paranal engineers in the analysis and operation of the different instruments and telescopes on site. It will also support ELT operations, which means there will be a significant increase in data volume.

From a technical perspective, Paranal’s data platform has several special requirements: it must always be available, even without internet access, so it needs to run on-premise within the observatory’s local network. There is also a large amount of telemetry data coming from the different instruments and systems operating across the telescopes, which makes streaming tools mandatory. On top of that, many Paranal engineers need to perform data analysis, modeling, and even develop their own applications to improve operational processes. Because of these requirements, as data engineers we need to build and deploy systems using technologies such as Python, Git, Kubernetes, Kafka/Spark, CI/CD, time-series databases, and more.

Now, on a more personal note, working in Paranal is a pretty crazy experience. Having to travel constantly to the Atacama Desert (like a miner) makes it quite fun, but at the same time working in shifts can feel a bit heavy sometimes. The weather doesn’t help either: humidity stays below 10% most of the year, and sometimes it drops below 5%, which dries out your lips and skin in a very aggressive way. On top of that, the residence is located at 2,500 meters above sea level, which doesn’t help much with sleeping—especially not when we play some casual football matches. Geographically, we’re also quite isolated from the rest of the world: the closest populated areas are Paposo (one hour away), Taltal (1.5 hours away), and Antofagasta (the regional capital, 2.5 hours away), with 500, 13,317, and 401,096 inhabitants respectively. Still, everything is worth it when you see the engineering behind each telescope. It’s truly impressive to witness state-of-the-art technology in areas like optics, electronic engineering, and mechanical engineering.

And I’m not going to pretend I’m suffering here—at Paranal we have all the comforts: a gym, a yoga room, a multi-sport court, a squash court, a climbing wall, a music room, a cinema room, a swimming pool, and even a sauna! On top of that, the views in Paranal are beautiful. The landscape is dramatic: countless reddish hills that, at sunset, create scenes filled with shadows and orange and purple tones. And of course, the most important thing: the people. In Paranal, there’s a sense of camaraderie that I hadn’t experienced in other workplaces. There are countless activities to get to know your colleagues, and I’ve met brilliant people who motivate you to keep growing and improving. Why is the environment like this? I don’t know. Maybe because it’s a European organization. Maybe because it has a fairly horizontal hierarchy. Or maybe because it’s a non-profit organization. Maybe all of the above.
